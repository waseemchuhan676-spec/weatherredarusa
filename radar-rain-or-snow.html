What Rain or Snow Shows

The map above is the precipitation-type layer — the same live NEXRAD radar picture as the reflectivity map, read as what kind of precipitation is falling. Every colored pixel is precipitation; the question is which kind. In summer the answer is almost always rain. In winter it depends on the temperature of the air the precipitation falls through: snow that falls through air below freezing stays snow, snow that falls through a warm layer melts into rain, and rain that falls into freezing air at the ground becomes freezing rain or sleet.

One honest limit first: the colors on this map are reflectivity, not precipitation type. No public radar feed colors rain blue and snow white — the live hydrometeor-classification and MRMS precipitation-type tiles are not served as public map tiles (checked 12 November 2026). What the colors do tell you is intensity, in dBZ, and intensity plus temperature is how forecasters tell rain from snow. The scale below is that method, and the map is live, so you can use it right now.

How to Read It: The Rain-or-Snow Scale

Snow is made of ice crystals, and ice reflects far less radar energy than liquid water, so snow echoes are weaker than rain echoes at the same rate. The table below turns any color on the map into a likely precipitation type — always together with the temperature. Three rules cover most cases: check the temperature first, remember snow rarely tops 35 to 40 dBZ, and watch for the bright band, the ring of higher reflectivity where snow melts into rain.

5 – 15 dBZ: Very light — drizzle or flurries; snow likely if below freezing
15 – 25 dBZ: Light — light rain or light snow
25 – 35 dBZ: Moderate — rain or steady snow
35 – 45 dBZ: Heavy — usually rain; heavy snow possible but uncommon; bright band lives here
45 – 55 dBZ: Very heavy rain — almost never snow
55+ dBZ: Severe — hail, not rain or snow

Two things to keep in mind. First, a 20 dBZ echo can mean light rain or moderate to heavy snow — the same value, two different types — which is why the temperature check matters more than the color. Second, colors are a guide, not a verdict: a red core in a winter storm is almost certainly rain, but only a National Weather Service warning tells you it is officially dangerous. Always pair the map with the live warnings feed on this page.

What Rain or Snow Is For

  • Winter travel: rain means wet roads, snow means slow roads, and freezing rain means stay home — the map plus the temperature tells you which one you are driving into.
  • School and work decisions: the rain-snow line decides closures, and it can run right through a county — watch which side of it you are on.
  • Shovel or umbrella: a green band at 30 degrees F is snow to shovel; the same green band at 40 degrees F is rain to wait out.
  • Freezing rain danger: ice on roads, trees and power lines is the most dangerous winter precipitation, and it looks like plain rain on radar — the temperature is the only warning.
  • Ski and snow conditions: steady snow at 25 to 35 dBZ means accumulating snow on the slopes; check the temperature to be sure it is snow, not rain.
  • Comparing storms: animating the loop shows where the rain-snow line is moving, and whether a storm is warming into rain or cooling into snow.

How Rain or Snow Is Made

The picture starts at more than 160 WSR-88D NEXRAD Doppler radar stations across the United States, jointly operated by the National Weather Service, the Department of Defense, and the Federal Aviation Administration. Each radar sends out pulses of microwave energy and listens for the echo. The strength of the returning energy at each point is the reflectivity, measured in dBZ.

In 2011 to 2013 the whole network was upgraded to dual-polarization: every radar now sends both horizontal and vertical pulses, so it can measure the shape and uniformity of the particles it hits. Snowflakes tumble and look nearly round to the radar, giving differential reflectivity near zero, while raindrops flatten as they fall and give positive values. The radar's hydrometeor classification algorithm combines reflectivity, differential reflectivity, correlation coefficient and specific differential phase and labels each echo as dry snow, wet snow, rain, heavy rain, graupel or hail.

The National Weather Service's MRMS system also produces a precipitation-type product, but it is not served as a public map tile feed — the hydrometeor-classification and MRMS precipitation-type tiles were checked on 12 November 2026 and are not available. So this page reads the live composite reflectivity mosaic through the rain-or-snow scale above, paired with the temperature rule, which is the same method forecasters use when a dedicated type layer is not in front of them. The live map is that composite reflectivity mosaic, served through RainViewer's radar API as an animated 2-hour loop, with the official NOAA NEXRAD base reflectivity feed available in the layer selector (via Iowa State University's IEM service). The 'Next 2 hours' view is different: it is simulated reflectivity from the NOAA HRRR weather model, read the same way, with the model's temperature profile deciding whether the precipitation will fall as rain or snow — a forecast rather than an observation.

What Rain or Snow Can't Tell You

  • The exact type from color alone: light rain and steady snow can share the same dBZ. The temperature profile, not the color, makes the call.
  • How much snow will fall: radar estimates snow from reflectivity, but the snow-to-liquid ratio varies — the 10-to-1 rule (10 inches of snow per inch of water) is only a rough average, from about 5-to-1 for wet snow to 20-to-1 or more for dry powder.
  • Rain versus freezing rain, from color alone: freezing rain and plain rain have similar reflectivity. Only the temperature at the ground separates them.
  • Weather behind the beam: the beam rises with distance and the Earth curves away, so far from a radar the beam can overshoot low snow — and hills can block it entirely.
  • Precipitation that never lands: virga — rain or snow evaporating on the way down — lights up the radar while the ground stays dry.
  • False echoes: the bright band of melting snow, ground clutter, and anomalous propagation can all paint colors where there is no weather. A lone bright patch on a clear day is usually one of these, not precipitation.

Rain or Snow FAQ

Forecasters combine three things: reflectivity, temperature, and dual-polarization data. Snow rarely returns more than about 35 to 40 dBZ, so a strong echo above 45 dBZ in winter is almost certainly rain or hail, not snow. Then they check the temperature profile: snow stays snow only while the air from cloud to ground stays below 0 degrees C (32 degrees F). Finally, dual-polarization data confirms it: snow has differential reflectivity near zero, under about 0.2 to 0.5 dB below 35 dBZ, while rain shows higher values, and a sharp dip in correlation coefficient marks the melting layer where snow turns to rain. No single clue settles it; the three together do.
Snow reaches the ground when the whole column of air from the cloud to the surface stays below 0 degrees C (32 degrees F). If the air aloft is cold but a layer near the ground is warmer, snow melts into rain before it lands. If a warm layer sits in the middle with cold air at the surface, the result is sleet or freezing rain instead. The surface temperature alone is not enough: snow can fall at 34 degrees F if the air above is cold, and rain can fall at 30 degrees F if the air above is warm. The National Weather Service hourly forecast gives the official precipitation type for your exact location.
Sleet is snow that melts in a warm layer aloft and then refreezes into ice pellets as it falls through cold air near the ground. Freezing rain is snow that melts completely and then freezes on contact with surfaces at or below 0 degrees C, coating roads, trees and power lines in ice. Both are more dangerous than plain rain or snow. On radar they look like ordinary rain, with similar reflectivity values, so you cannot tell them apart from the colors alone. The temperature profile and the National Weather Service winter weather warnings are what identify them.
Dual-polarization radar sends out both horizontal and vertical pulses, so it can measure the shape and uniformity of what it hits. Snowflakes tumble as they fall, so their differential reflectivity stays near zero, under about 0.2 to 0.5 dB when reflectivity is below 35 dBZ, while flattened raindrops give higher values. The correlation coefficient stays high, near 0.97 to 1.0, for uniform snow or uniform rain, and dips sharply in the melting layer where snow turns to rain. The radar's hydrometeor classification algorithm combines these clues and labels each echo as dry snow, wet snow, rain, heavy rain, graupel or hail.
Mixed precipitation looks patchy and noisy: reflectivity values jump around, often 25 to 45 dBZ, with no clean pattern, and the correlation coefficient drops in the melting layer where snow is turning to rain. You may see a bright band, a ring or band of higher reflectivity around the radar where snow is melting. Several types can fall in the same county at the same time, rain on one side of a line and snow on the other, so the map shows where precipitation is falling while the temperature profile decides which type it is.
