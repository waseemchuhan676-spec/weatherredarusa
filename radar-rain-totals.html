What Rain Totals Show

The map above answers a different question from the live radar loop: not "is it raining now?" but "how much rain has already fallen?" Every colored area is the radar-estimated rainfall total for the window you picked — the last 1 hour or the last 24 hours — in inches, from NOAA's MRMS (Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor) system. A blank map is good news: it means no measurable rain fell in that window, not that the map is broken.

Two windows, two uses. The 24-hour view is the one for "how much rain did we get yesterday?" — the total for the whole day, across every storm that passed through. The 1-hour view is the one to watch while a storm is overhead: it shows only the last hour, so a fast-growing downpour lights up while yesterday's rain fades away. Switch between them with the buttons above the map.

How to Read It: The Rain Totals Scale

Colors on this map are inches of rain, not radar intensity — darker and warmer colors mean more water on the ground. The scale below is the official NOAA MRMS QPE scale for the 24-hour view. The 1-hour view uses the same colors with tighter breaks, because an inch in one hour is a downpour while an inch in a day is just a wet day.

0.01 – 0.50 in (24 h): Trace to light — blue; a light shower or drizzle
0.50 – 2.00 in (24 h): Light to moderate — green; a steady rainy day
2.00 – 5.00 in (24 h): Heavy — yellow to orange; flooding starts to be possible
5.00 – 9.00 in (24 h): Very heavy — pink to red; flash flooding likely somewhere in the area
9.00 – 16.00 in (24 h): Extreme — purple; a major flood event
16.00 – 24.00+ in (24 h): Record-level — white to pale blue; historic rainfall

On the 1-hour view the same colors mean smaller amounts: blue up to about 0.2 inches, green up to 1 inch, yellow up to 2 inches, red from 3 inches, purple from 4 inches, and white above 6 inches in a single hour. Two things to keep in mind. First, always check which window you are looking at — the same green that means "a rainy day" on the 24-hour view means "raining hard right now" on the 1-hour view. Second, totals are an average over about a 1-kilometre square, so one street can be wetter than the color over it suggests.

What Rain Totals Are For

  • Flash flooding: watch the 1-hour view during a storm — deep yellow, orange or red over your area means rain is falling fast enough to matter, and the 24-hour view shows whether the ground is already soaked.
  • River flooding: the 24-hour total over a whole river basin is what pushes rivers up — a few inches spread over a watershed matters more than a heavy burst over one town.
  • Farming and gardening: how much water the soil actually got, and whether to water or wait — a quarter inch wets the surface, an inch soaks the roots.
  • Drought watch: compare 24-hour totals day to day to see which places are getting rain and which are being missed.
  • Travel: roads that flooded yesterday can still be closed today — the 24-hour map shows where the water fell.
  • Comparing storms: the same storm can drop half an inch in one county and five inches in the next — the totals map shows the gradient the live loop cannot.

How Rain Totals Are Made

The picture starts at more than 160 WSR-88D NEXRAD Doppler radar stations across the United States, jointly operated by the National Weather Service, the Department of Defense, and the Federal Aviation Administration. Each radar measures reflectivity — how strongly precipitation bounces its microwave pulses back — and turns it into a rain rate with a reflectivity-to-rainfall relationship (a Z–R relationship). The National Weather Service's single-radar one-hour product (N1P) uses its Precipitation Processing System algorithm for this step.

MRMS then mosaics all those radars onto one 1-kilometre grid and adds the rain rates up over time: the last hour for the 1-hour total, the last 24 hours for the 24-hour total. The layer on this page is the National Weather Service's MRMS QPE Image Service, which is radar-only — a separate gauge-corrected MultiSensor QPE also exists, blending in about 7,000 rain gauges, but this service shows the radar estimate alone. The totals update once an hour, usually within a few minutes after the hour. The National Weather Service also publishes a storm-total precipitation product (NTP), which accumulates from the start of one storm until it ends, rather than over a fixed window.

What Rain Totals Can't Tell You

  • The exact amount at your house: radar estimates the average over about a 1-kilometre square; your gauge measures one small bucket. The two rarely match to the hundredth of an inch.
  • Snow depth: totals are liquid-water equivalent — snow shows as the water it would make if melted, not as inches of snow. The rough 10-to-1 rule (10 inches of snow per inch of water) varies from about 5-to-1 for wet snow to 20-to-1 or more for dry powder.
  • Rain before the window: the 24-hour view only covers the last 24 hours, and the 1-hour view only the last hour — rain from two days ago is not in either total.
  • Rain the radar cannot see: the beam rises with distance and the Earth curves away, so far from a radar the beam can overshoot low rain — and hills can block it entirely. Coverage also thins over the oceans and outside the US radar network.
  • Hail and melting snow: hail looks like extremely heavy rain to the radar, and melting snow creates a bright band that looks like very heavy rain — both inflate the totals.
  • Rain that never lands: virga — rain evaporating on the way down — lights up the radar and adds to the total while the ground stays dry.
  • Whether it will flood: totals are only half the story — saturated ground, steep terrain, small streams and blocked drains decide whether a given amount of rain becomes a flood.

Radar measures rain high in the sky and estimates how much reaches the ground, while a gauge measures exactly what fell into one small bucket — so the two rarely match to the hundredth of an inch. The usual reasons are distance from the radar, where the beam passes well above the ground and can miss low rain or overshoot it; the beam being partly blocked by hills or buildings; bright-band errors where melting snow looks like very heavy rain; hail, which looks like extreme rain to the radar; and rain that evaporates before landing, called virga. The MRMS totals on this page are radar-only, so a nearby gauge is the better check for one exact location, while the map is the better picture of the whole storm.
Storm total precipitation is the National Weather Service radar product (NTP) that adds up all the rain from the start of one storm until it ends, however long the storm lasts — a 6-hour storm gets a 6-hour total, a 3-day storm gets a 3-day total. The 24-hour total on this page is different: it is a fixed window, always the last 24 hours, no matter how many storms passed through. Use the storm total to judge one storm, and the 24-hour total to compare today with yesterday or to check the last day's rain at a glance.
