What Echo Tops Show

The map above answers a different question from the live radar loop: not "is it raining now?" or "how hard is it raining?" but "how tall is the storm?" Every colored area is the height of the top of the precipitation core — the highest point where the radar still sees an echo of 18 dBZ or stronger, the National Weather Service definition of an echo top. Heights are in thousands of feet (kft). A blank map is good news: it means no precipitation strong enough to measure a top, not that the map is broken.

Low tops (blue, under about 15,000 feet) mean shallow showers and light rain. Tall tops (yellow, orange and red, 35,000 to 60,000 feet and beyond) mean deep thunderstorms with powerful updrafts holding rain and hail high in the sky. The taller the top, the stronger the storm — which is why forecasters watch echo tops when a storm is growing, and why pilots use them to judge whether they can fly over a storm or must go around it.

How to Read It: The Echo Tops Scale

Colors on this map are heights in thousands of feet, not radar intensity — warmer colors mean a taller storm. The scale below is the official Iowa State IEM scale for the NEXRAD Enhanced Echo Tops product, with one step per thousand feet from 1,000 to 70,000 feet.

1 – 10 kft: Shallow showers — blue; tops barely above the low clouds
11 – 15 kft: Moderate showers — light blue to cyan; tops reaching the mid-levels
16 – 20 kft: Developing thunderstorms — green
21 – 30 kft: Strong thunderstorms — dark green; tops near the middle of the troposphere
31 – 40 kft: Very strong storms — yellow; tops pushing toward the tropopause
41 – 49 kft: Severe storms likely — orange; large hail possible
50 – 60 kft: Intense storms, often supercells — red; large hail and tornadoes possible
61 – 70+ kft: Extreme overshooting tops — pink to magenta; among the tallest storms on Earth

Two things to keep in mind. First, height is not the same as intensity — a tall, thin storm can drop less rain than a shorter, wider one, so check the rain totals map for how much rain actually fell. Second, the scale tops out at 70,000 feet, the limit of the NWS echo tops product — the very tallest storms reach that ceiling, they do not go beyond it on this map.

What Echo Tops Are For

  • Finding the strongest storms: the tallest tops mark the strongest updrafts — scan the map for the red and orange cores and you have found the storms that matter most.
  • Severe weather: forecasters watch tops above about 35,000 to 40,000 feet combined with strong reflectivity; when a 50 or 60 dBZ core is held high, large hail is likely, and the 30 dBZ echo-top height tracks lightning activity closely.
  • Aviation: pilots use echo tops to judge whether they can fly over a storm — a top above the aircraft's altitude means go around, not over. Never use an echo top as a cloud top: the visible cloud runs 5,000 to 10,000 feet higher.
  • Watching a storm grow or die: tops rising fast over a few scans mean a storm is strengthening; tops collapsing mean the updraft is weakening and the storm is dying.
  • Winter weather: snow bands have low tops — lake-effect snow often tops out under 15,000 feet — so blue on this map in winter still means a real storm, just a shallow one.
  • Comparing storms: two storms can look the same on the reflectivity map and be very different heights — the echo tops map shows which one is the deep, dangerous one.

How Echo Tops Are Made

The picture starts at more than 160 WSR-88D NEXRAD Doppler radar stations across the United States, jointly operated by the National Weather Service, the Department of Defense, and the Federal Aviation Administration. Each radar scans the sky in a volume coverage pattern — a series of tilts from near the horizon up to high elevation angles — measuring reflectivity at each height.

At every point on the map, the Enhanced Echo Tops (EET) algorithm scans the vertical column from the top down and finds the highest altitude where reflectivity reaches 18 dBZ, interpolating between the two nearest elevation scans. The Iowa State University IEM then mosaics the echo tops from all the radars into one national picture, on a 1-kilometre grid, about every 5 minutes — that mosaic is the tile layer on this page. The legacy NEXRAD echo tops product used a 4-by-4-kilometre grid and 5,000-foot steps; the enhanced product resolves heights to 1,000 feet.

What Echo Tops Can't Tell You

  • Cloud tops: the visible cloud is usually 5,000 to 10,000 feet higher than the echo top — radar cannot see the smallest cloud droplets, so the echo top is always lower than the cloud top.
  • How much rain will fall: height is not intensity — a tall, thin storm can drop less rain than a short, wide one. For rainfall amounts, see the rain totals map.
  • The exact height far from a radar: the beam widens with distance and the Earth curves away, so tops can be over- or underestimated far from a radar site, and low precipitation can be overshot entirely.
  • Clouds with no precipitation: no echo does not mean no cloud — thin cirrus anvils and developing cumulus may be invisible to the radar while still being there.
  • Hail size directly: high tops plus strong reflectivity suggest hail, but only a Severe Thunderstorm Warning or a ground report confirms it.
  • Whether a storm is severe: height is one clue among many — warnings combine reflectivity, velocity, lightning, and spotter reports, so always follow the warning, not the height alone.

Echo Tops FAQ

An echo top is the height of the top of a storm's precipitation core, in thousands of feet. The National Weather Service defines it as the height of the highest radar echo of 18 dBZ or stronger in a vertical column — in other words, how high the rain, snow or hail inside a storm reaches. On this map, blue means a shallow echo top under about 15,000 feet, green and yellow mean a developing or strong thunderstorm, and orange, red and pink mean a very tall, severe storm reaching 40,000 to 60,000 feet and beyond.

The strongest storms — supercells — reach 50,000 to 60,000 feet and beyond, and the scale on this map runs to 70,000 feet. Tops that tall mean a very powerful updraft is holding rain and hail high in the sky, which is why forecasters watch echo tops closely when a storm is growing.
Echo tops alone do not prove hail, but they are one of the best clues. Forecasters look at how high the strong core of a storm is held up: when a 50 or 60 dBZ echo — the very heavy rain and hail core — reaches high into a storm, large hail is likely being held aloft by the updraft. A 30 dBZ echo top held high is also closely linked to lightning. A tall echo top plus strong reflectivity is a much stronger hail signal than either one by itself — but only a Severe Thunderstorm Warning or a ground report confirms hail.
